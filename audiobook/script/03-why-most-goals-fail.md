# Chapter Three: Why Most Goals Fail

For many, there is a massive divide between what they say and what they do; this stems from two factors: the underestimation of effort required and the misalignment with what we actually desire.

Most of us have honest intentions, but our day starts, and we become unconscious. Sometimes we black out for months just to find ourselves staring into a lake on a random Sunday afternoon, remembering all our good intentions from the start of the year. In this hyper-stimulating world, it is easy to lose track of time; without mindfulness, a new way of being can not be implemented.

For the more conscious, the problem lies in a lack of a deeper understanding of oneself. We may strive for money with the hidden goal to feel secure or become a successful manager to feel important. We are often ashamed of the underlying feeling and cover it with complex stories of wants and needs, hopes and dreams. This is not necessarily a problem if it serves you well, unfortunately, for many of us, it manifests as a toxic slurry that drags us into a dark place. When the goals are reached, a further feeling of despair consumes us, realising that it did not solve our internal problems, and we are left disillusioned with life.

As you listen, I ask only that you resist the urge to separate yourself from the eight point three billion other humans. We are all liable to fall for these two tricks of the mind.

## What is the goal of goals?

It is believed that the Babylonians made the first New Year's resolutions about four thousand years ago at the start of their spring barley harvest. Fast forward to today, and millions of people set New Year's resolutions every year. According to Goal-Setting Theory, developed by Locke and Latham, goals can direct attention, energise effort and increase persistence.

## Quitter's Day

Unfortunately, most goals are quickly abandoned, the most famous being the lofty New Year's Eve resolutions. Strava analysed over eight hundred million activities logged in 2019 and predicted that people were most likely to give up their 2020 fitness resolutions on January the nineteenth, cleverly coined "Quitter's Day." Richard Wiseman's 2007 study of three thousand participants found that eighty-eight percent of those who set resolutions fail to achieve them, despite fifty-two percent being confident of success when they started. Outside of New Year's resolutions, we set specific goals throughout the year, from savings targets to social media metrics. Whatever the type, they all seem to be plagued by an underestimation of effort.

## Planning fallacy

It's not a matter of intelligence, some of the smartest people in the world entrusted with billions are falling for the same underestimation of timelines. The term "Planning Fallacy" was introduced by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1979 in their paper "Intuitive Prediction: Biases and Corrective Procedures." They realised that we tend to underestimate the time required to complete a goal, even when we have past experiences of failure. Roger Buehler's studies add that we more accurately predict the required time when planning for others than for ourselves.

The reasons are complex but centre around three points. First, memory biases: we misremember how long tasks took in the past. Second, planning simulations: when we think of the future, we often can only simulate a perfect world without setbacks. And third, uniqueness bias: Bent Flyvbjerg's megaproject research identified the tendency to see one's own goals as unique.

Flyvbjerg analysed over sixteen thousand large projects and found that ninety-one and a half percent go over budget, over schedule, or both. Fewer than one percent are completed on time, on budget, and deliver promised benefits. A few well-known examples. The Sydney Opera House was estimated at seven million dollars; it actually cost a hundred and two million. The Channel Tunnel was predicted at four point nine billion pounds; it actually cost more than ten billion. And Denver International Airport ran a two hundred percent cost overrun.

## Play silly games, win silly prizes

Now, some would argue that being overly optimistic is a good thing, as it can get us started, but unfortunately, setting shitty goals has shitty consequences. Research by Kasser and Ryan found that people who strongly valued financial success reported worse psychological health, and a meta-analysis by Dittmar and colleagues confirmed that materialism is connected with lower life satisfaction, risky health behaviours, and negative self-image.

Even if the timeline is correctly estimated, sticking with the wrong goals is challenging and can be dangerous. "Goal Contents Theory" describes the difference between intrinsic and extrinsic goals. Intrinsic goals include personal growth, meaningful relationships, community involvement, and physical health. Extrinsic goals include financial success, fame, and image and appearance.

Knowing the negatives of extrinsic goals, why are we so often drawn to them? A meta-analysis by McComb and colleagues found that being exposed to social media content showing a higher social status negatively affects the users' well-being and contributes to further feelings of depression. Social media users consistently set more extrinsic goals. The same comparison happens offline, where seeing what others have can increase your desire to "keep up with the Joneses", an American saying for the need to match the neighbours when they buy a new car or go on a luxury holiday.

The fundamental problem with extrinsic goals is that they fail to satisfy the psychological needs they are unconsciously meant to address. Behind the surface goal of wealth sits the underlying need for security. Behind fame, the need for recognition that one matters. Behind the perfect image, the need for self-acceptance. And behind status symbols, the need to be recognised as a competent person.

## The rat race offers no cheese at the finish line

Another danger is the "Arrival Fallacy," which makes many believe that achieving a specific goal will bring lasting happiness. Post-Olympic depression shows where this leads. Olympic superstars are often depicted as the very definition of drive and determination, yet a large portion of them suffer from major mental difficulties right after their big victory. They often operate in a constant state of desire that fuels their relentless action, and when that desire is seemingly fulfilled, the effects can be devastating.

## Effortless goals

In response to these and other challenges, I propose the concept of Effortless Goals, a method to help set more sustainable goals that improve one's well-being. People often say, "You can do anything you set your mind to". This is, in my humble opinion, total bullshit. That is why Effortless Goals are designed to cut the crap and try to be as close to reality as possible. Think of it as harm reduction for goal-setting: clean needles and proper dosing will help reduce casualties, but do not necessarily solve the underlying problems. The deeper work comes in later chapters.

The method has five steps. Each one acts as a filter, the goals that make it through all five are likely worth pursuing. Goals that don't should probably be abandoned.

### Step one: Surface goal

Start by writing down what you think you want. Don't edit yourself yet, just capture the goal as it naturally appears in your mind.

For example: "I want to make a million dollars." "I want to be a successful entrepreneur." "I want to be promoted to director." "I want to start a podcast."

This is your surface goal, the thing you'd tell someone at a party if they asked about your ambitions.

### Step two: Radical self honesty

For most, this is one of the hardest steps, as digging deep into the subconscious takes time and energy. Ask "why do I want this?" and keep drilling down until the answer feels uncomfortably honest. For example, "I want financial security" can be a safe version of "I want to stop feeling anxious that I'm one mistake away from losing everything".

A few more examples. "I want to be promoted" may mask "I want respect" or "I want to prove my worth." "I want to get fit" may mask "I want to feel attractive" or "I want control over my life." "I want to start a business" may mask "I want autonomy" or "I want to prove I'm capable."

Keep asking why, as if you are trying to understand somebody else's point of view. "I want to start a podcast." Why? "To share my ideas." Why? "Because I have insights people should hear." Why? "Because I want recognition for my thinking." Why? "Because I want to feel like I matter and my ideas are respected."

Keep going until the answer feels vulnerable or slightly embarrassing. True desires almost always fall into one of four categories. Security: safety, stability, freedom from fear or anxiety. Acceptance: belonging, being valued, mattering to others, love. Autonomy: control, freedom, self-determination, independence. And competence: feeling capable, respected for skills, mastery, being "enough."

Important: please be kind to yourself! These are difficult topics, and you may not like what you find. Have patience and realise that we are all human. The point of this step is to take away the weight of being dishonest to yourself so that you can start moving towards a better future.

### Step three: Create a goal that serves your desire

Now translate your underlying desire into a concrete and actionable goal.

For example, your desire is "I want to feel that I am heard and my ideas are respected." Possible goals: "Publish ten well-researched articles in my field." "Speak at three industry conferences." "Host monthly dinners with five thoughtful people in my industry."

Your goal should be specific enough that you can estimate how long it will take, know when you've achieved it, and identify a strategy to get there.

### Step four: Minimize

Once you've created your goal, ask: "What's the smallest version of this goal that would still satisfy my desire?" Choose the version that's fastest, most direct, and most aligned with your actual life situation. Don't pick "write a best-selling book" if "become an expert in my community" would satisfy the same desire. Remember that the only thing that matters is how you feel about your situation. Identify the best bang for your buck.

The discipline is found in making the goal smaller and learning how to squeeze the maximum out of it. If you don't, then step five will destroy your hopes and dreams. Furthermore, you need to make sure it can be achieved with one single strategy. If your goal requires multiple different strategies, then in my opinion, it's a vision, not a goal.

For example: going from a thousand euros a month to ten thousand a month is one strategy. From ten thousand to a hundred thousand a month is a different strategy. From a hundred thousand to a million a month is a radically different strategy. Each strategy shift is essentially a new goal.

### Step five: The reality multiplier

Now take your reframed, minimized, single-strategy goal and estimate the total effort required: how many hours per week you'll dedicate to it, and how many weeks, months or years it will take.

Express this as a time estimation. Two hours a week for six months: fifty hours. Ten hours a week for one year: five hundred and twenty hours. Forty hours a week for two years: four thousand hours.

Account for difficulty! If something is completely new to you or requires intense focus, the same clock hours cost more psychological effort, and you will not be able to do as many per week.

Now comes the really shitty part. Multiply your total effort estimate by ten. Yes, ten times. I know... fuck that. But unfortunately, this is your realistic baseline, and what you should actually expect.

Now ask yourself: If this goal takes ten times more effort than I thought, do I still want it? The results are often devastating. That six-month side project of fifty hours now becomes a five-year endeavour of five hundred hours.

If ten times kills all your motivation, there may be one of three problems. One: the desire isn't strong enough. Return to step two. Two: your time commitment is too low. Adjust your time allocation if possible. Three: you didn't minimize properly. Return to step four.

Before abandoning a goal, see if you can increase your weekly time commitment. For example, you estimate that learning Spanish takes three months at four hours a week; this results in a two-and-a-half-year estimate after applying the Multiplier. Alternatively, you envision eight hours a week and a month and a half of work, which would result in a roughly one-year commitment.

Increasing weekly time only works if the goal actually responds to more hours; some things have natural pacing limits. On the flipside, some goals benefit greatly from extra weekly hours, exponentially speeding up completion time. Either way, ramp up slowly. Even a goal that survived all five filters can crush you if you throw yourself at it full speed from day one. Start at a pace that feels almost too easy, and build from there. If even your maximum weekly commitment yields a timeline that kills motivation, go back to those three problems.

Consider two versions of the same type of goal. The minimized version: building a small business in six months turns into five years. The overly ambitious version: building a large business in two years turns into twenty years.

Before the multiplier, the gap between six months and two years seems modest. After it, the gap is between a large chapter and the remainder of your career.

Now, if ten times feels trivially easy, your original estimate was probably a fantasy. Revisit your timeline with more honesty before applying the multiplier. On rare occasions, goals take significantly less effort than even your original estimate, because you underestimated your existing capabilities or the goal was simpler than you thought; luck plays the biggest role in the early completion of goals. If this happens, great. The Reality Multiplier protects you from the far more common scenario: massive underestimation.

If your goal comes out of the five steps successfully, then to you I say "Happy Hunting."
