## Chapter Seven: me.md

### Why Create a me.md File?

Before we start creating your own version of "ME" I want you to think about what happens when you open a conversation with an AI system. You type a question, and the AI responds based on statistical patterns drawn from billions of words. It sounds articulate and thoughtful, as if it knows you, but it doesn't.


Without context, AI gives you the same generic advice it would give anyone. "Have you considered journaling?" "It might help to talk to a therapist." "Remember to practice self-care." These aren't wrong, exactly, just useless. It's like asking a doctor for help without telling them where it hurts.

The previous six chapters asked you to look at yourself more closely than most people ever do. Right now all of that lives in your head, maybe in some scattered notes. Me.md puts everything you've uncovered into one document that you, and any AI system you hand it to, can read.



### The Sections

The document has six sections: five that hold what you've uncovered, and a revision log that tracks how the document changes. If this feels like a lot, relax: each of the first five draws directly from a chapter you've already read, so you're organizing what you've found rather than starting from scratch.

```
  $ tree ~/me.md

  me.md
  │
  ├── Where I Came From
  │   Context that shaped who you are.
  │
  ├── My Foundation
  │   Your Five Pillars, the non-negotiables.
  │
  ├── Where I Am Going
  │   Directional narratives, not goals.
  │
  ├── My Relationship with Gain and Loss
  │   What you gained, what it cost.
  │
  ├── What I Need From AI
  │   How AI should interact with you.
  │
  └── Revision Log
      Track how this document evolves.
```

&nbsp;

\needspace{4\baselineskip}
**Section 1: Where I Came From**

Your background, your formative experiences, and the patterns you recognized in the first two chapters: where you came from, what shaped you, where you are now. Skip the polished version you'd put on a resume or a dating profile and write the real one, including the parts you usually leave out.

**Section 2: My Foundation**

The current state of your Five Pillars: what's working, what keeps collapsing, where you started versus where you are now. Add your cascade patterns: which pillar drags the others down when it slips, and which one pulls them forward when it improves.

**Section 3: Where I Am Going**

This is your Chapter Three and Five material: the goals that survived the Effortless Goals filter, if any did, the core desires you found when you kept asking "why?", and the seeds you created for your pillars and life domains. "I am becoming someone who..." for each area that matters to you.

**Section 4: My Relationship with Gain and Loss**

How do you relate to winning and losing? What did your Post-Achievement Audit reveal? What did your gains actually give you versus what you expected? What did loss teach you that success couldn't?


**Section 5: What I Need From AI**

This might be the most practically useful section. How do you want AI to talk to you? What should it push back on? What should it absolutely not do?

If you respond well to directness, say so. If motivational cheerleading makes you want to throw your phone, say that. If you need AI to flag when you're running old scripts, write it down. If there are areas where you want help and areas where you don't, make it explicit.

This section shapes every future conversation. Without it you get the default, which is designed to be safe and pleasant, and that usually means fucking useless.


**Section 6: Revision Log**

Keep a revision log. Nobody's checking; the point is that the changes become a record of your own evolution. "March 2027- updated Section 3, old seed no longer resonates, replaced with one that reflects where I actually am." "June - rewrote Section 1 after processing shutting down the company."

Most people won't maintain their me.md. They'll write a version, feel satisfied, and let it gather dust, and that's okay. Even a single honest version, written once, is more self-knowledge than most people ever organize. It will still serve you when you paste it into a conversation two years from now, just not as well as one you've kept current.


### How to Build It

Don't try to write the whole thing in one sitting; that's the same "overhaul everything at once" trap I warned about with the pillars. Start where the friction is lowest and let momentum build. For most people, that's Section 3, your goals and seeds.

Section 5 (what you need from AI) is worth spending real time on. Think about the frustrating interactions you've had with AI: what went wrong, what you wished it had done instead, which tone works for you and which doesn't. Be specific. "Don't be overly positive" is okay. "When I'm catastrophizing, call it out directly instead of validating the spiral" is better. Think of this section as instructions for a very capable but very new assistant who wants to help but doesn't know you yet.

The first version is a draft, incomplete and imperfect, and that's fine. A rough document with honest material is far more useful than a polished one full of what you think you should say. Nobody is grading it; the only measure of quality is honesty. If a section makes you cringe a little to write, you're probably doing it right. Leave sections blank if you're not ready. A gap is better than filler, and you can come back to it later.


### Before You Share It With AI

Be aware that when you use a cloud AI service, your conversation is sent to servers controlled by that company. What happens next depends on their policies. Some providers store your conversations indefinitely. Some use them to train future models, meaning your words become part of the system's learning material. Some allow employees to review conversations for quality and safety. Policies vary between providers, change over time, and are often buried in terms of service that nobody reads.

Also consider how these models were built in the first place. Most large language models were trained on enormous amounts of data scraped from the internet, much of it without the consent of the people who created it. This is documented and still being litigated, and it shows they are more than willing to steal.

That said, most of us pour deeply personal information into email services, messaging apps, and social media without a second thought. People confess secrets over Snapchat and send medical details through unencrypted email boxes with 12345 as their password. The document you've built deserves more care than that, but there's no need to be more paranoid about AI than you are about everything else.

The alternative is a local AI model that runs entirely on your own machine, so nothing leaves your computer: no servers, no storage policies, no training data contributions, no third-party access. Models that run on a powerful laptop are already capable enough to work with your document in meaningful ways, and they're improving fast.

The tradeoff today is capability. The largest cloud models are generally more capable than those that run locally. That gap is closing, and for many conversations a local model is more than sufficient, but for some the extra capability might matter. You get to decide where that line is for you. I only share my full document on my own local machine, a technical luxury that I think is worthwhile for me.

At minimum, before you paste your document into any AI system, know the answers to these questions: Does this provider store my conversations? Can I delete them? Are they used for training? Can I opt out? If you can't find clear answers, assume the worst.


### How to Use It

The most immediate use is pasting it into the beginning of any AI conversation, whether career advice, emotional support, or decision-making. One practical note: don't always use the full document. The complete document is a lot of text, and flooding a conversation with too much context can actually degrade the quality of the output. Share the sections that are relevant to what you're working on. If you're making a career decision, the AI doesn't need your full health history.

Take a simple question: "I'm thinking about changing careers. What should I consider?"

Without your document, you get generic advice about researching industries and updating your resume. With your document, you get a response that knows your pattern of fusing identity with achievement, that knows your sleep collapses when you're stressed, that knows you're prone to chasing external validation when you feel insecure, and that knows your seed is "I am becoming someone who creates from genuine interest rather than proving worth." The advice that comes back from that foundation is a different species entirely.

Beyond AI conversations, the document works as a decision-making filter. When a choice comes up, pull up the document and ask: does this align with my seeds? Does this serve my actual desires or my inherited scripts? Does this strengthen my pillars or undermine them?

Share it with a therapist or coach if you have one. Most professionals spend the first several sessions trying to understand who you are. It's not a replacement for the relationship, but it's a hell of a starting point.

Use it with people you trust, not as a "here, read my life story" but as a reference when you're making decisions together, working through problems, or trying to explain where you're coming from.

The document doesn't tell you what to do; it tells you who you are and where you're pointed. The decisions still require judgment, courage, and occasionally dumb luck. The document just makes sure you make them with your eyes open.
