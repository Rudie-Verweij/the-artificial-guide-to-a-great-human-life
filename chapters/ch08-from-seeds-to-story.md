## Chapter Eight: From Seeds to Story

Your me.md is done, or done enough. This short chapter is optional: why the stories we tell about ourselves matter, and one structure for telling yours.

---

### Humans ARE Narrative

Dan McAdams spent decades studying how people construct their identities. His conclusion is that your identity *is* your internalized and evolving life story. You don't just tell stories to yourself, you ARE story.

McAdams distinguishes between redemption sequences (narratives that move from bad to good, where suffering transforms into growth) and contamination sequences (good to bad, where positive experiences are spoiled). Adults with redemption narratives show significantly higher generativity (the drive to contribute to future generations) and wellbeing. The story you tell about your life literally shapes your psychological state.

Jonathan Adler took this further. He tracked 47 adults writing personal narratives before psychotherapy and after every session, roughly 600 narratives total. What changed first was agency, the sense of being in charge of your own life. In his words: "We found that the stories actually changed BEFORE people's psychological well-being changed. It was as though people were narrating a new version of their lives, and then a week or two later, their well-being would sort of catch up with the new story."

In a study by Dunlop and Tracy of recently sober alcoholics, 83% of those whose stories of their last drink contained redemption sequences were still sober at follow-up. Of those without, 44% were.

The same facts can tell different stories. A failed startup can be the end of a humiliating chapter or the painful beginning of finding what actually matters; where you draw the line between chapters determines whether your story reads as decline or growth.


### Kishōtenketsu

The specific shape your narrative takes depends on your culture, your relationships, and who you are.

The hero's journey, the classic story structure where a protagonist leaves their ordinary world, faces trials, and returns transformed, assumes you had a stable baseline to leave. For many of us, there was no stable baseline. It was chaos from the start. Three-act structure requires conflict as the organizing principle, rising tension, climax, resolution. But what if your life wasn't a battle to be won?

There's a structure from East Asia that works differently. It originated in Chinese four-line poetry during the Tang Dynasty and spread to Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. In Japanese, it's called Kishōtenketsu. It reflects a different relationship to conflict.

**Ki: Introduction.** Establishes context and tone, with no inciting incident required. It simply invites you into your world, however it was.

**Shō: Development.** Deepens understanding. Events unfold without building toward confrontation; this is life happening, the texture of experience before the shift.

**Ten: The Twist.** The heart of the structure: a perspective shift rather than a crisis, an insight that recontextualizes everything before it. One writing instructor describes it as "a steep spike, not a gradual ramp."

**Ketsu: Reconciliation.** Harmonizing the elements rather than resolving them. Open endings are valid; the story indicates that life continues beyond what we see.

Applied to a life, Ki and Shō are your past: where you came from and how things unfolded. The Ten is the shift, and the Ketsu is where your seeds point, without a fixed ending. If you've been building your pillars, planting seeds and writing your me.md, you're probably in your Ten right now.

If you want, write your own story into Section 6 of your me.md, in this structure or any other, and revisit it now and then, because narrative identity keeps changing. If the writing turns into rumination, or brings up more than you can handle, stop, go back to the pillars, and get support from friends or professionals.
